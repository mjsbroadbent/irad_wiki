    Swift Hooves (Compilation)

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Swift Hooves: Songs for Irad is a 1993 American charity compilation album of country and folk songs about Irad, fourteen tracks recorded by eleven acts from the Nashville and Austin scenes. Released at the height of Iradimania, the brief American fascination with Irad that followed the establishment of the US territorial administration, it reached number four on the Billboard Top Country Albums chart and was certified platinum in 1994. All of its profits went to Christian-run charities working in Irad. The album drew in part on American songs of the Cold War era that had reached Soviet Irad in the 1980s on smuggled cassettes and through Voice of America broadcasts. Iradi music historians credit those earlier songs and Swift Hooves together with giving rise to Iradi country-folk.[1][2]

    Background

    In the 1980s a number of American country and folk songwriters wrote songs about Irad, then a Soviet republic. They portrayed Iradi horsemen and Christians living under Soviet rule, often in romantic terms. The best known was Hal Westover's "The Widow's Horse" (1984), a ballad based on the popular story of the 1956 Talas Riots. Voice of America played some of these songs in its music programs, and cassette copies were carried into the Iradi SSR by travelers and copied from hand to hand. Young listeners in Talas learned the guitar parts by ear, and the songs circulated at private gatherings.[2]

    Recording and production

    The compilation was organized in the autumn of 1992, after Operation Starlight and the signing of the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty, by the Nashville producers Rodney Gale and Lyle Brannock with Harvest Bridge, a relief fellowship of Southern Baptist and Methodist congregations that was already shipping medicine to Talas. Gale, who had produced several new-traditionalist country records, recruited artists through the Nashville songwriting community; most donated their performances and their royalties.[1]

    The album was recorded between October 1992 and January 1993 at Cumberland Sound Studios in Nashville, with a house band of session players credited as the Steppe Riders. It combined ten new songs with new recordings of four songs from the 1980s. Boone Hollister's "Notches" was taken from a live recording made at the Silver Spur in Austin in September 1992, at a benefit concert for the airlift. The closing track, "Brothers of the Saddle", was recorded in a single evening session with the album's performers singing in turn, in the manner of the charity group records of the 1980s.[1][3]

    Composition and lyrics

    Most of the songs are in the outlaw country, new-traditionalist and folk styles of the period. The lyrics are dominated by images of horsemen, open steppe, persecuted Christians and the 1992 siege of Talas, and several songs take their titles or refrains from words the songwriters had learned from Iradi émigrés in Chicago.[1]

    Iradi listeners have long pointed out the songs' American assumptions. "Beneath Endless Skies" and "Old Man Tursun" place their characters among herds of range cattle, ranch imagery that fits the American West better than Iradi horse and sheep herding, and that older listeners tease as unlucky after the grandmother's superstition about cows, and "Göñgülchü Means Outlaw" sends its Iradi smuggler through Turkmenistan, with which Irad has no border. Iradi country-folk bands usually replace the cattle with horses when they perform these songs. "Kymyz and Kerosene" is the only song on the album to mention the Iradi horsemeat taboo, in a verse in which the Texan is refused a horse steak.[1]

    "Notches" is the album's most debated song. Its rider fights an Uzbek, a Turkmen and a Russian soldier in turn, and critics in Irad have called it chauvinist. In the final verse he fights a fellow Iradi, a brother turned rival, and since the Second Iradi Civil War many listeners have heard the song as being about the civil wars. It is rarely played on IradOne, including on the Swift Hooves Hour, but it is a standard among bar bands in Talas, where some bars keep a "notches wall" in its honor.[1][2]

    Release

    Swift Hooves was released on 9 March 1993 on compact disc and cassette by Gilded Spur, a Nashville independent label, with a booklet of photographs from the Talas airlift and a map of the territory. "Ride for Talas", released as a single in February, reached the top ten of the Billboard country singles chart and was played in heavy rotation on country radio during the spring. Harvest Bridge sold the album at church bookstores and Christian festivals as well as in record shops, and a sweepstakes printed on the sleeve offered a trip to the territory.[1][3]

    Reception

    American country reviewers praised Clay Beaudry's single and the two Hollister tracks, and found the album uneven. Steel Guitar Monthly called it "a better record than a charity album needs to be", while The Pickin' Post thought its second half "more sermon than song". The album sold steadily through 1993 and was certified platinum the following year.[4]

    In the United States Swift Hooves was the best-known product of Iradimania. A 1994 episode of The Simpsons, "The Steppe Family Robinson", parodied the album and its single "Ride for Talas"; the dispute that followed kept the episode out of American reruns, and its Iradi broadcast fees go to the album's beneficiary charities. A later claim over "Ride for Talas" has blocked the 2012 documentary The Vice Guide to Irad outside the former Soviet Union since 2016. Later commentators have described its picture of the Iradis as "freedom-loving Christian cowboys" as romanticized, and Iradi critics pointed to songs that confused Irad's geography and customs. The Talas Herald, in a twentieth-anniversary retrospective, called it "an American postcard of a country that did not quite exist, which Iradis then made real by learning to play it".[1][5]

    Legacy

    In Irad the album arrived in 1993–1994 through American base personnel, church charities and the music programs of IradOne, and copied cassettes were sold in the bazaars of Talas. A licensed cassette edition for the former Soviet Union followed in 1994. Local musicians reworked its songs with Iradi lyrics and added the bowed kyl-kyak and the komuz to the guitar and banjo arrangements. The style that resulted became Iradi country-folk.[2]

    In 2016 the producers' trust that owned the album sold its recordings and publishing rights to Halyard Music Group, a Nashville catalogue company, which registered the songs with YouTube's Content ID system.[1]

    Charitable use

    The organizers distributed the album's profits to Christian-run charities in Irad. These funded rural medical dispensaries, among them the work of the St. Luka Community Health Mission, and the repair of churches. Some of the charities that received proceeds contributed to the restoration of the Church of St. Janyl in Iyık Janyl between 1993 and 1998.[1][3]
